# Validity-threat catalog

A generalized catalog of recurring threats to validity in empirical evaluations of a
system, tool, or intervention — abstracted from a real applied-research planning
exercise, with all project-specific identifiers removed. Each threat carries a
detection obligation and a mitigation obligation for whoever runs the study.
**Mitigation obligations are not claims that a threat has been handled** — most have a
residual, and the residual is stated rather than hidden.

Not every threat applies to every study. Screen the list against the actual design and
keep only what is live; record why any dropped threat does not apply rather than
silently omitting it.

| ID | Threat | Why it commonly arises | Detection obligation | Mitigation obligation | Typical residual |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **T-01** | **Builder-as-evaluator.** The team that owns, directs, and built the system under study is also running the evaluation of it. | Nearly universal in applied/product research: the people with the deepest knowledge of the system are the people most invested in a favorable result. | Record every point where the team touches data collection, coding, adjudication, or analysis, and check whether any single decision was made by the builder alone. | Independent coders and raters not involved in development; blind analysts to condition; pre-registered analysis; independent replication of at least the primary result; an explicit positionality statement. | Cannot be eliminated in a single-team project. Must be **declared**, not managed away. |
| **T-02** | **Novelty / Hawthorne / demand characteristics.** Participants know they are trying something new, in a study run by people who want it to work. | Any first-exposure design; amplified by a small, invested rater or participant pool. | Post-task probes about perceived study purpose; compare early vs. late sessions; look for uniform positivity in subjective outcomes. | Neutral framing and materials; an equally unfamiliar comparator where possible; longer exposure before measurement; pre-declare that positive subjective ratings alone are not a finding. | Genuine novelty effects and genuine benefits are not separable in a single short session. |
| **T-03** | **Comparator mismatch (the bundling problem).** A system usually bundles several distinct things at once — access to material, structure over it, generated content, and a display/interface layer, for example. A single comparator that varies all of them cannot attribute an effect to any one part. | Whenever the system under test is a multi-component product rather than a single manipulation. | For every planned contrast, state which components it varies and which it holds constant. | Include a matched-static comparator wherever an access confound is possible; build comparator materials to genuinely match; report the bundle-attribution limit in the results, not only in limitations. | Full decomposition needs more conditions than most designs can carry. |
| **T-04** | **Corpus/data circularity.** The team's own data, logs, or ledgers serve as instrument, object, and outcome measure all at once. | Common when the evaluation data and the system's own working data overlap. | Trace each dataset's role: is it calibration, evaluation, or outcome? Flag any artifact serving more than one role. | Evaluation data the team has not processed; reference standards built independently of system output; leakage detection between splits. | Some self-generated results are intrinsically self-audits and must be reported as such. |
| **T-05** | **Gold-set artificiality and provenance circularity.** Reference-set items are authored to illustrate their own labels rather than drawn from real, naturally occurring material. | Common when a gold set is built quickly by the same team, especially for underrepresented categories. | Per-item provenance audit before any reuse; separate synthetic from naturally-occurring items in every reported result. | Use real, naturally occurring items for any construct whose claim depends on realism; report synthetic and real subsets separately; never let a synthetic-item result stand in for real-world performance. | A classifier validated on prose written to illustrate its own categories is validated against its own design. |
| **T-06** | **Reference-data license defect.** Reused reference data rests on an undocumented or informal grant rather than a written license. | Common with reused third-party fixtures or corpora. | Per-file license-status check before any research or publication use. | Obtain a durable written grant, or exclude affected files and use only cleanly-licensed material, or re-derive the construct on independently licensed data. | A collaborator's or third party's license status may be outside the team's unilateral control; disclose as a conflict item. |
| **T-07** | **Version/configuration drift mid-study.** A dependency (model, provider, pipeline) changes version while the study runs, or different environments run different versions simultaneously. | Especially likely with hosted/commercial model or API dependencies, and with parallel CI vs. production configurations. | Log the exact model/provider, prompt version/hash, retrieval or pipeline configuration, seeds where supported, content hash, code commit, and feature-flag state for every evaluated run; re-run a fixed stability subset periodically. | Freeze versions where the provider permits; pre-commit to a revalidation protocol on any change; treat a mid-study change as a design event to report, not a footnote. | Commercial or third-party dependencies can change without notice; freezing is best-effort. |
| **T-08** | **Deployment-state ambiguity.** Code presence is not feature availability — a capability may exist in code but be flag-gated off (or selectively on) in the environment actually studied. | Common in feature-flagged systems where documentation and actual runtime configuration can diverge. | Capture the actual configuration state of the study build, per session, from the running system — not from example config files or documentation. | State the studied configuration explicitly in every claim; never generalize from a research build to "the product." | Any single result is a result about one configuration. |
| **T-09** | **Measurement reactivity.** Think-aloud protocols, event instrumentation, and observation change the behavior being measured. | Any behavioral or process outcome (what someone checks, opens, or verifies). | Compare instrumented against minimally instrumented sessions where feasible; probe awareness of logging in debrief. | Minimize visible instrumentation; disclose logging honestly at consent without cueing specific behaviors; treat reactivity as a stated bound on behavioral outcomes. | Consent requires disclosure; disclosure creates awareness. Largely irreducible. |
| **T-10** | **Differential attrition and missingness.** Multi-session or longitudinal designs lose participants non-randomly. | Any design with delay, follow-up, or multiple sessions. | Report attrition by condition with reasons; test whether missingness relates to baseline characteristics or early outcomes. | Pre-specify the missing-data approach before data collection; use non-coercive retention procedures; report both completer-only and full-sample analyses. | Missing-not-at-random cannot be fully ruled out from the data alone. |
| **T-11** | **Prior-knowledge (or baseline-ability) confounding.** A relevant prior characteristic plausibly drives both willingness to participate and the outcome, and interacts with the treatment. | Common wherever the intervention is meant to help people who lack some prior skill or knowledge. | Measure the relevant baseline independently of any self-rating the system itself produces; report its distribution by condition. | Stratify or model as a moderator; report the range over which any claim holds. | Self-report baseline measures carry their own validity problem. |
| **T-12** | **Self-selection of volunteers.** Volunteers for a study of a new tool are plausibly more favorably disposed, more technically confident, and more motivated than the target population. | Any unpaid or open-call recruitment. | Collect and report baseline attitudes and relevant background; compare respondents to the target population on what is knowable. | Use broad recruitment channels; report the sampling frame and refusal information honestly; bound generalization claims to the recruited population. | Cannot be corrected by weighting alone in a small study. |
| **T-13** | **Single-domain / single-corpus generalization.** Results from one domain, format, or scope-bounded graph would not necessarily transfer elsewhere. | Whenever material or scope is drawn from one domain out of convenience. | State domain, format, and scope for every result; check whether any claim silently generalizes beyond it. | Multi-domain sampling where affordable, else an explicit single-domain bound stated in the claim itself. | Multi-domain expansion multiplies reference-set cost. |
| **T-14** | **Expert-panel anchoring and consensus-as-truth.** Small expert panels converge; adjudication can manufacture agreement; consensus is not the same as correctness. | Any small, closely-coordinating rater panel. | Record independent ratings before any discussion; report pre-adjudication agreement separately from post-adjudication; track whether one rater's view systematically prevails. | Independent rating before discussion; a documented adjudication protocol; retain substantive alternatives and their rationales; use disagreement-preserving standards where plurality is real. | Where experts genuinely disagree, there may be no single right answer to measure against, and the design must be able to say so. |
| **T-15** | **Construct drift between the claim and the measure.** The measure quietly becomes the construct's definition rather than an approximation of it. | Any gap between how a claim is stated at a high level and how a specific instrument operationalizes it. | Maintain the question→construct→evidence→measure map as a living document and re-check it at analysis time against the claim it was built for. | Any measure change after preregistration is reported as a change with its reason; the map is re-audited before results are written up. | Some constructs may have no adequate measure, which is itself a finding about the construct. |
| **T-16** | **Ceiling, floor, and task artificiality.** Short, session-sized tasks on excerpted or simplified material may not discriminate, and may not resemble real-world use. | Any lab-scale task designed for feasibility rather than ecological realism. | Pilot for score distributions and task duration before any main run; inspect for compression at either end. | Calibrate task difficulty in pilot; use realistic materials where feasible; report the artificiality bound in the claim. | Ecological validity and experimental control trade off against each other with no free option. |
| **T-17** | **Perception–performance dissociation.** Participants may prefer, and rate as more helpful, a condition that actually performs worse. | Common wherever fluency or ease-of-use is confounded with actual quality (the "fluency illusion"). | Always measure both perception and performance; test the relationship between them rather than assuming alignment. | Pre-commit to reporting dissociation as a primary finding; never substitute a subjective outcome for an objective one in a headline claim. | A real phenomenon, not a measurement artifact to be engineered away. |
| **T-18** | **Instrument defects producing false results.** A mechanical or automated check can fail because of the checker, not the thing being checked. | Any automated verification, extraction, or scoring pipeline. | Reproduce known-correct cases before trusting any mechanical measure; test both branches of any normalization before recording a negative. | Build ground-truth reproduction cases into every automated measure; adopt a documented policy that a negative result triggers instrument inspection first, not acceptance. | Undiscovered defect classes remain possible even after a known one is fixed. |
| **T-19** | **Multiplicity and analysis flexibility.** Many outcomes, many conditions, many subgroups create room to search for a favorable result after the fact. | Any design with more than a small, pre-specified set of outcomes. | Pre-register primary and secondary outcomes, contrasts, and subgroup analyses; report every declared outcome including null ones. | Apply multiplicity correction within declared families, or explicit exploratory labeling that survives into the summary/abstract; keep a public analysis plan. | Preregistration constrains reporting, not thinking. |
| **T-20** | **Accessibility-driven exclusion presenting as a null effect.** If an interface is unusable with assistive technology, affected participants' outcomes reflect the barrier, not the mechanism under study. | Any participant-facing interface not verified with a manual accessibility pass. | Collect assistive-technology use as a participant characteristic; analyze outcomes and dropout by it. | A manual accessibility audit **before** any participant study; accessible alternatives for every study material and instrument; report accessibility barriers encountered as results. | Automated scans alone do not establish usability. |
| **T-21** | **Data leakage between calibration and evaluation sets.** Document, entity, network, and near-duplicate leakage all threaten a split's validity. | Any evaluation with a development/held-out split, especially over relationally connected data (citations, social graphs, dependency graphs). | Automated leakage detection on all applicable axes before freezing any split; report what it found. | Split at the correct unit (which may be a broader entity than the item itself); freeze the test set; re-run detection after any corpus change. | Densely connected data may make perfect separation impossible; the residual overlap must then be quantified, not ignored. |
| **T-22** | **Interpretive over-reach in both directions.** Forcing an interpretive claim into a falsification template, or letting an interpretive claim escape all challenge. | Any project mixing empirical and interpretive claims. | Classify every claim by type before assigning challenge criteria; audit for empirical vocabulary attached to interpretive claims and vice versa. | Apply the claim-type rule: falsifiers and estimands for suitable empirical claims; counterevidence, rival interpretations, limiting cases, defeaters, and revision conditions for interpretive ones. | Some claims are genuinely mixed and need both treatments, stated separately. |

## How to use this catalog

1. **Screen, don't copy wholesale.** Go threat by threat and mark: applies /
   doesn't apply / applies partially, with a one-line reason.
2. **For every threat that applies**, fill in the detection and mitigation obligations
   with study-specific detail — who runs the detection check, when, and what the
   mitigation concretely looks like in this design.
3. **State the residual explicitly** in the write-up. A mitigation section that implies
   a threat has been fully solved, when the catalog's own residual column says it
   cannot be, is itself a validity problem — an overclaim about the study's own rigor.
4. **Re-screen after any design change.** A comparator swap, a recruitment channel
   change, or an instrumentation change can activate a threat that was previously
   inapplicable.
